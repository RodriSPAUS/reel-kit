# Writing scripts that people watch to the end

Short videos live or die in the first two seconds. People decide very fast whether to keep watching, so the script does most of the work.

## The hook (first line)

- Say the most surprising thing **first**. Not "Today I'll tell you about octopuses", but "An octopus has three hearts."
- Make it concrete: a number, a picture in the head, a contradiction.
- The hook must be true and the video must pay it off. Clickbait gets people to leave, and the apps notice.
- Show it on screen too: many people watch muted. The kit's cover frames and subtitles help here.

Hook shapes that work:
- **Surprising fact:** "Sharks are older than trees."
- **Question they can't answer:** "Why is the sea salty but rivers aren't?"
- **Contradiction:** "The bigger the animal, the slower its heart."
- **Stakes:** "This tiny mistake costs people thousands."

## The body

- **One idea per video.** If you have three ideas, that's three videos (a series!).
- **One idea per scene,** one short sentence per line.
- Give each scene something to *see*: a drawing, a number counting up, a comparison.
- Add a small twist or "but…" near the end to keep people watching.

## The ending

- End fast. Once the point is made, stop.
- A question ("Which animal should I do next?") gets comments; "Follow for one fact a day" tells people what they'll get.
- A loop works well: an ending that leads back into the hook makes people rewatch.

## Length and wording

- 20–45 seconds is a good target: about 60–110 words.
- Short words. Read it out loud; if you stumble, the voice will too.
- Write numbers the way they're said if the voice reads them oddly ("three hundred million", not "300M").
- Cut every word that isn't needed. Then cut a few more.

## Check before recording

- [ ] Hook is the first line and is surprising on its own
- [ ] Every fact has a source (as a `#` comment in `script.txt`)
- [ ] One idea, clearly paid off
- [ ] Under 110 words
- [ ] Read out loud once
